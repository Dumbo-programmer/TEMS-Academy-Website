How to work, not just what to read.

All subjects All tiers

The two-pass reading habit

Most lost marks start before the first line of working. Read the problem once for the situation, once for the constraints.

  • First pass: what is happening, and what is being asked?
  • Second pass: underline every condition, unit and restriction.
  • Write down what is given and what is unknown before choosing a method.
  • If you feel stuck, re-read the last sentence of the question.
All subjects All tiers

Keep a solution journal

A journal turns a pile of solved problems into a set of methods you can actually reuse.

  • Record the key idea, not the whole solution.
  • Name the method in your own words.
  • Note the trigger that made the method work.
  • Revisit the entry a week later and try the problem again from scratch.
Mathematics Gamma

Draw before you calculate

A clean diagram is often the shortest path into a geometry or counting problem.

  • Draw large, label every point and mark equal lengths and angles.
  • Look for triangles, circles and parallel lines before writing algebra.
  • For counting, draw the cases; do not try to hold them in your head.
  • Redraw if the picture becomes crowded.
Physics Alpha

Units, limits and sanity checks

A physics answer is not finished until the units and the size of the answer make sense.

  • Carry units through the calculation, not just into the final line.
  • Check the limiting cases: what happens if a mass is zero or a time is very large?
  • Estimate the answer before calculating it.
  • If the answer is wildly different from the estimate, find the error before moving on.
Chemistry Alpha

The mole map

Almost every quantitative chemistry problem is a route between mass, moles, concentration and gas volume.

  • Convert everything to moles first.
  • Use the balanced equation to move between substances.
  • Convert back to the unit the question asks for.
  • Check significant figures and units at the end.
Biology Alpha

Structure, function, evidence

When a biology question looks like recall, look for the structure–function link or the data it wants you to interpret.

  • Name the structure and its function in one sentence.
  • Link the function to the environment or scale where it matters.
  • Quote the data or observation that supports the claim.
  • Distinguish correlation from causation in experimental data.
All subjects Sigma

How to review a mock paper

The review is where most of the improvement happens. Marking the score is the least useful part.

  • Classify every lost mark: concept, method, arithmetic or presentation.
  • Rewrite the full solution for each problem you missed.
  • Re-attempt the paper after a week without looking at your notes.
  • Track whether the same error type reappears.
Mathematics Sigma

Write the proof you wish you had read

A proof is an argument for a reader, not a transcript of your thoughts.

  • State what you are proving and the method you will use.
  • Introduce every symbol before using it.
  • Separate the cases explicitly; do not hide them in a sentence.
  • End by connecting the conclusion back to the claim.

A broad general biology text with strong ecology and evolution coverage.

Study habits

Six habits that compound.

None of these require a new book. They require a notebook and a week of consistency.

  1. 01

    Attempt the problem before reading the solution, even if you only write down what is given.

  2. 02

    Write full solutions, not just final answers. The reasoning is the thing being trained.

  3. 03

    Keep an error log and classify mistakes: concept, method, arithmetic or presentation.

  4. 04

    Revisit a problem after a week. If you cannot restart it, the method is not yet yours.

  5. 05

    Timebox practice papers so that finishing becomes a skill, not an accident.

  6. 06

    Explain a solution out loud to someone else. Gaps in the argument become obvious.
